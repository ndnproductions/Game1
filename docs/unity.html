Unity Field Manual

Unity will not make your game look good. It draws exactly what you hand it — the polish comes from art, animation and sound you buy or commission, and from a juice pass you build yourself.

First, the correction

What the engine does and doesn't do

What Unity gives you

A renderer, an animation system, particles, audio mixing, touch input, a UI layout system, and one-button export to both stores. All the plumbing you would otherwise write.

What it does not give you

Taste, art, characters, sound, or motion design. A Unity game built from default shapes looks exactly like a game built from default shapes.

What actually makes it look expensive

Character art with real animation, a coherent UI kit, sound on every interaction, and tweened motion on everything that moves. That is the work — the engine only makes it possible.

The pipeline · in order, each step depends on the last

Prototype to store, A to Z

  1. 01Install and set up~2h Unity Hub, then the current LTS release. Create a 2D (URP) project and add the iOS and Android build modules during install — adding them later means a long re-download.
  2. 02Port the rules~6h Translate game.ts into plain C# classes. This is the cheap part: the file is pure logic with no drawing or input in it, so the stage curve, suspicion economy and pocket rules move over almost line for line.
  3. 03Rebuild the screen~8h Sprites for marks and loot, a Canvas for the job list and coat, and raycast-based tap handling. Placeholder shapes are fine here — do not wait for art to make it playable.
  4. 04Buy the art~4h A character pack for the crowd, a UI kit for panels and buttons, and item icons. Sources and licences are in the table below. Pick one kit and stay inside it — mixing three art styles is the fastest way to look amateur.
  5. 05Animate the crowd~6h A walk cycle, an idle, and a head-turn for the alert state. Sprite sheets are simplest; Spine or DragonBones give smoother skeletal animation and are what most polished 2D mobile games use.
  6. 06Sound design~3h A lift, a stash, a spotted sting, a fail, a stage-clear fanfare, and one loop of street ambience. Sound is the cheapest perceived-quality upgrade there is — a silent game feels broken.
  7. 07The juice pass~8h DOTween on every movement, particles on stash and fail, TextMeshPro for type, screen shake, and haptics. This single phase is the difference between "student project" and "shippable".
  8. 08Menus and flow~5h Title screen, stage map, the clear and caught screens, settings, and save data. Unglamorous and always underestimated.
  9. 09Monetization~4h Ad mediation through AppLovin MAX or Unity LevelPlay, plus the bribe-the-guard offer as an IAP or rewarded video. Skip this entirely if you are pitching publishers — they rip it out and install their own.
  10. 10Test on real devices~5h An old cheap Android and any iPhone. Watch frame rate, build size, battery, and safe areas around the notch. The editor lies about all four.
  11. 11Store submission~6h + waiting Developer accounts, icons, screenshots, descriptions, privacy declarations, age rating, then review. Budget days, not hours, for the first submission on each store.

Licensing

The part that gets games pulled

LicenceWhat you may do
CC0Anything, including selling. No credit required. The safest thing to build on.
CC-BYCommercial use is fine, but you must credit the author somewhere in the app.
CC-BY-NCNot usable. The NC means non-commercial — a game with ads or IAP is commercial.
Asset Store EULAUse in your games freely. You may not resell or redistribute the asset itself.
Two rules that will save you

Never pull art or audio from an image search, another game, or a video — that is how apps get removed from stores and how small studios get legal letters. And keep a licences.txt in the project listing every asset, where it came from, and under what terms. You will not remember in six months, and a publisher will ask.

Unity itself is free until you pass $200k in revenue, so the engine is not the cost — your hours are. And one requirement catches everyone out: a new Google Play personal account must run a closed test with 12 testers for 14 straight days before it can publish to production. Start recruiting those twelve people long before the build is ready.

Sequencing

Do not start this yet

Every hour spent porting is an hour not spent finding out whether the game is fun. The TypeScript prototype is the design document, and it is cheaper to change there than in C#.

Port when two things are true: the stage curve feels right when you play it, and you want to keep playing past stage five without being the person who built it. Until then, the honest answer to "should I open Unity" is no.
